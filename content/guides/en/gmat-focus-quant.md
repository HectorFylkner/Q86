GMAT Focus Edition replaced the previous GMAT in 2023, and the quant section changed more than most summaries suggest. This piece covers what is actually measured, how the score is set, and why the most common study advice is built for an exam that no longer exists.

## The format, briefly

Quantitative Reasoning in GMAT Focus is **21 questions in 45 minutes** — a little over two minutes per question on average. Every question is Problem Solving. Data Sufficiency still exists on the exam, but it moved to the Data Insights section.

That is the single largest change from the old exam, and it is often missed: if you are drilling Data Sufficiency to improve your quant score, you are now training for a different section.

Geometry has been removed from the quant section. What remains is arithmetic and algebra in the sense GMAC uses those words — in practice, numbers and their properties, equations and inequalities, ratios and rates, plus counting, sets and basic statistics.

The exam is **question-adaptive**, and in Focus Edition you may go back and change up to three answers per section. That is a real tactical difference: a question you are unsure of can be flagged and left, instead of eating four minutes.

## The score scale

Each section is reported on a **60–90** scale. The total runs from 205 to 805 and always ends in a five, which makes it easy to tell whether someone is quoting a Focus score or an old one.

The name Q86 comes from here: 86 on the quant section sits in the upper part of the scale, roughly where the most selective programmes stop looking at the number. It is a target, not a promise.

One thing that confuses people: Focus percentiles are not comparable to the old ones. A Q49 on the old exam and a Q86 on Focus are different measurements on different scales with different norming groups. Conversion tables exist, but they are approximate, and GMAC's own is the only one worth consulting.

## The four fundamental skills

GMAC describes the quant content as four skill areas rather than as a list of topics. That is a useful division, because it matches how the questions are actually built:

| Fundamental skill | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Rates, ratio, percent | Proportions, ratios, percent chains, speed and work, mixtures, interest |
| Value, order, factors | Prime factorisation, divisibility, remainders, parity, signs, evenly spaced sets |
| Equal/unequal, algebra | Linear systems, quadratics, inequalities, absolute value, functions and sequences |
| Counting, sets, series, probability, statistics | Combinatorics, overlapping sets, series, probability, mean and median |

The point of the division is diagnostic. "I am bad at maths" cannot be acted on. "I lose 40% on value/order/factors, and nearly all of it is remainders and parity" can be acted on in a week.

## Two minutes a question is an average, not a target

45 minutes over 21 questions is 2 minutes 8 seconds. The distribution is not even in practice: a question you recognise takes 40 seconds, and one you do not takes four minutes if you let it.

That is why timed sets matter more than per-question speed. What you need to train is the decision to leave a question, not the ability to compute faster.

## What separates good practice from a lot of practice

The most common study pattern is to do questions, mark them, and move on. That produces volume but not information: you know how many you got right, and not why the others were wrong.

A missed answer has one of six causes, and they call for different fixes:

1. **Content gap** — you did not know the rule. Fix: read the chapter.
2. **Setup error** — you knew the rule but translated the words wrong. Fix: drill the translation step on its own.
3. **Calculation error** — the setup was right, the arithmetic was not. Fix: write intermediate steps.
4. **Misread** — you answered a different question from the one asked. Fix: reread the final sentence before marking.
5. **Time pressure** — you could have, but did not have time. Fix: train the decision to leave.
6. **Guess** — you had no way in. Fix: same as 1, but start again from the chapter.

Without separating these, every fix becomes "do more questions", which is rarely the right answer.

## Where to start

If you do not know where you stand: take a short diagnostic that covers all four fundamental skills, and record the *error type* for each miss, not just whether it was right. Twelve questions is enough to point at the area that should get this week's attention — not to predict a score, but that is not what you need right now.
