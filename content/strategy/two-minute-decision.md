# The two-minute decision

## The budget

The section is $21$ questions in $45$ minutes — an average of $2$:$08$ per question, no calculator, no partial credit. But the average is not the plan. Easy questions exist to give time back; hard ones exist to take it. The trainer's benchmarks, the same ones the timed-set analytics grade you against, are: difficulty $1$ → $1$:$25$, difficulty $2$ → $1$:$40$, difficulty $3$ → $2$:$05$, difficulty $4$ → $2$:$30$, difficulty $5$ → $2$:$50$. Every worked example in the chapters carries one of these targets, so by the time you sit a timed set the numbers should feel like body knowledge, not arithmetic.

Two failure shapes cost more points than any content gap. A **time sink** is a question that ran half again over its benchmark — one sink is survivable, two force rushed guesses at the end. A **rushed wrong** is a miss in under half the benchmark — you had the time and spent it elsewhere. The timed analytics name both after every set. Above the $655$ level, fixing these two shapes moves your score faster than learning new math.

## The first thirty seconds

Every question gets a route decision by $0$:$30$: straight algebra, number testing, backsolving, estimation, or a property shortcut (parity, units digits, divisibility). The chapters' trigger cues exist precisely to make this decision fast — "at least one" means complement, "variables in the choices" means test numbers, "which value of $x$" with clean numeric choices means backsolve. Reading the answer choices is part of reading the question: their spread and shape often decide the route for you.

Not choosing is itself a choice — the default of "start manipulating and see" is how time sinks begin. If at $0$:$30$ you cannot name a route, that is a signal in itself: mark it, guess, move. You just bought $2$:$00$ for two questions you can actually win.

## The checkpoint at the target

When you hit the question's pace target, look up. The question is not over, but the plan is due for review: either you can see the finish from here — two or three mechanical steps, no new ideas — or you cannot. If you can, finish. If you cannot, you get exactly one route change: algebra stalling on a "must be true" becomes number testing; an equation with ugly fractions becomes backsolving from the choices. One change, not a tour.

The rule that protects you is: never be surprised twice by the same question. Stalled once, switched, stalled again — that question has told you what it costs, and the price is a string of rushed guesses at the end of the section. Bail.

## Bailing well

A bail is not a zero. Thirty seconds of elimination usually beats a blind fifth: kill choices with the wrong sign, the wrong parity, the wrong magnitude, or a shape the stem forbids. Then pick from what survives, record your confidence honestly — a bail is a *guess*, not a *lean* — flag it, and leave. The confidence tag is not bookkeeping: a flagged guess that happens to land right still goes back into your study loop, and an unflagged one silently poisons your accuracy read.

The arithmetic of bailing is lopsided. Spending $4$:$00$ to maybe rescue one hard question costs you two end-of-section questions almost surely. The exam does not reward heroism; it rewards the player who loses the right questions.

## Review & Edit

The real exam lets you bookmark questions and, at the end of the section, change up to $3$ answers. The section simulator reproduces this exactly — including the justification gate: it will not accept an edit without a named reason, and the edit ledger records every change and whether it helped.

Use the mechanic the way the data says it works: edits that fix a *misread* — you return and the question asks something different from what you answered — are overwhelmingly profitable. Edits that re-litigate a computation from memory are coin flips. So the in-section discipline is: flag on a specific doubt ("did it say integer?"), not on general unease, and bank enough time to actually return. Three edits is a scarce resource; a section where you wanted five means the first pass was too fast, not that three is too few.

## Where the time actually goes

After every timed set, read the pacing panel before the accuracy panel. Average time against benchmark per difficulty tells you where your $45$ minutes actually went — most plateaus are not "too slow everywhere" but one difficulty band eating double budget. The sinks list, worst first, tells you which stems held you; the rushed-wrong list tells you which points you gave away to fund them. One is the disease, the other the symptom, and the cure is always the same: an earlier, cleaner two-minute decision.
