# Pacing and the Clock

Forty-five minutes, twenty-one questions. That is roughly $128$ seconds
each, and the average is the least useful number in the room — nobody
spends $128$ seconds on anything. What matters is the shape of the
distribution: easy questions bank time, hard questions spend it, and one
question spending four minutes eats the bank for three others.

## Benchmarks, not averages

The platform holds a per-difficulty benchmark: about $100$ seconds at D2,
$125$ at D3, $150$ at D4, $170$ at D5. Those are targets for a question you
can actually do, not speed limits. Two derived flags matter more than the
raw times:

- **Time sink** — you spent more than half again the benchmark. The
  question was harder than you read it as, or your first path was wrong and
  you kept walking it.
- **Rushed wrong** — you answered incorrectly in under half the benchmark.
  You did not slow down where slowing down was the whole job.

A section can be lost to either. Sinks are the more common failure at the
top of the scale, because a strong student's instinct is that one more
minute will crack it.

## The sixty-second decision

The trainable skill is not speed. It is the decision you make at sixty
seconds, which is the point at which you know whether you have a path.

At sixty seconds, one of three things is true:

1. **You have a path and you are executing it.** Continue. Finishing is
   worth more than the clock you will spend.
2. **You have a path and it is ugly** — three substitutions deep, numbers
   getting worse. Stop and look for the chapter's speed move once. If none
   appears in fifteen seconds, take your best structural guess and leave.
3. **You have no path.** Guess and move, immediately. This is the cheapest
   decision available and the hardest to make, because sixty seconds feels
   like an investment. It is not; it is spent either way.

The cost of a wrong answer is one question. The cost of a four-minute wrong
answer is that question plus the two you rush afterwards.

## Reading your own pacing

After a timed set, look at the pacing read before the score. Two questions:

- **Where did the time actually go?** Sinks are named individually. If the
  same subtopic keeps appearing, that is not a pacing problem wearing a
  pacing costume — it is a content gap, and drilling it untimed is the fix.
- **Are the rushed-wrong ones concentrated at the end?** That is the bank
  running out, and it means the fix belongs earlier in the section, not at
  the point where the symptom shows.

Timing failures cannot be trained untimed. If your misses tag as time
pressure, the prescription is timed sets, not more drilling.
