# Reading your misses

## Six kinds of wrong

Every miss on this platform gets filed under one of six error types, and the discipline of filing honestly is worth more than most hours of new study. The types, precisely:

- **Content gap** — you did not know the fact or method. Shown the solution, your reaction is "I've never seen that."
- **Setup error** — you knew the method and built the wrong equation, diagram, or case list. The reaction is "of course — I modeled it wrong."
- **Calculation error** — right model, wrong arithmetic or algebra somewhere in the middle.
- **Misread** — you answered a different question than the one asked: solved for $x$ when it wanted $x + y$, gave the count when it wanted the probability, missed the word "integer" or "not".
- **Time pressure** — you knew how, and the clock made you abandon or rush it.
- **Guess** — you bailed deliberately. Not a failure if it was flagged and chosen; a serious signal if it wasn't.

The types matter because they have *different cures*, and mixing them up buys the wrong medicine. Drilling more content fixes exactly one of the six.

## Find the first wrong line

The post-mortem habit that separates improvement from repetition: locate the **first written line that is wrong** — not the last one, not the answer. Everything after the first wrong line is contaminated; everything before it is fine and needs no work. The whiteboard post-mortem automates this — photograph your scratch work and the coach names the line — but the skill transfers to paper: reconstruct where the truth left the page. A miss whose first wrong line is the very first line is a setup error or misread no matter how much arithmetic followed; a miss whose first ten lines are perfect is not evidence you "can't do" the topic.

## What each type prescribes

- **Content gap** → back to the chapter, and not to skim: reread the specific core idea, redo its check, then drill the subtopic at low difficulty until the method is boring. This is the only type where more content study is the answer.
- **Setup error** → slow the first thirty seconds. Your translations are outrunning your reading. Rehearse the stem-to-equation move in the chapter's trigger cues, and write the defining equation *before* simplifying anything.
- **Calculation error** → the fix is mechanical, not conceptual: fewer steps done mentally, cancel before multiplying, write intermediate values, run the two-second audits (units digit, magnitude, sanity band). Chronic calculation errors on one subtopic usually mean you're using a longer method than the chapter's speed moves allow.
- **Misread** → build the re-read reflex: before committing an answer, look once more at the final sentence of the stem and confirm the thing you computed is the thing it asked for. Misreads are also the highest-value Review & Edit flags — "did it say integer?" is exactly the doubt worth an edit.
- **Time pressure** → nothing is wrong with your math; something is wrong with your two-minute decision. Reread that note, then look at your sinks list: time pressure at question $18$ is usually caused at question $9$.
- **Guess** → audit the bail, not the answer. Right question to give up on? Eliminated what was eliminable? Flagged it? If yes three times, this miss cost you nothing — it was purchased time.

## Calibration: guess, lean, lock

Every attempt records a confidence — *guess*, *lean*, or *lock* — and the analytics compare your confidence to your accuracy. Read that panel like this: a missed **lock** is worth three ordinary misses, because it means a belief you trust is false — those go to the front of the study queue. **Leans** landing near $50\%$ are honest; leans landing near $90\%$ mean you're under-calling yourself and burning Review & Edit flags on questions you'd already won. **Guesses** landing well above chance mean your eliminations are better than you think — trust them and bail faster.

## The redo queue is the log

Misses come back on a schedule — $+2$ days, then $+7$, then $+21$ — and the day-$21$ return is a *cold solve*: no notes, no memory of the explanation, just the question again. That schedule is the platform's error log, and it only works if you let it: don't re-solve a miss the same evening from memory of the solution — that rehearses recall of an explanation, not the skill. The queue's question is not "do you remember this problem" but "has the first wrong line moved."

## The weekly read

Once a week, ask one question of the analytics: *which error type dominates, in which skill group?* One dominant pair — say, setup errors in Rates/Ratio/Percent — sets the week's focus; the daily plan already weights toward weak subtopics, and your job is to bring the matching cure from the list above. Chase one dominant type at a time. A student who eliminates their leading error type every two weeks runs out of leading error types — that is the whole strategy.
