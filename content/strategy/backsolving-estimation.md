# Backsolving, estimation & choice leverage

## The choices are part of the question

Five answer choices are not five candidate endings — they are data. Before choosing a route (see *The two-minute decision*), scan them for three things: **spread** (far-apart values invite estimation; tight values forbid it), **shape** (integers? fractions? one negative? all multiples of $3$?), and **form** (numeric, algebraic, or "closest to"). Thirty percent of route decisions are made by the choices alone. A stem that looks like heavy algebra above choices that are small clean integers is usually a backsolve; a monstrous computation above choices spread across orders of magnitude is an estimate wearing a costume.

## Backsolving

When the question asks "what is $x$" and the choices are concrete numbers, you can run the problem backwards: assume a choice is the answer, push it through the stem's conditions, and see if the story balances. Numeric choices on the exam are sorted, so start with **B or D**, not C: if B fails *and you can tell which direction it failed in*, you have eliminated three choices in one test (B plus everything on the wrong side). One more test decides among the rest. Two substitutions, done.

Backsolving wins when checking is cheaper than solving — age problems, "a number is chosen such that…" setups, anything where the forward direction needs an equation you'd have to build but the backward direction is plain arithmetic. It loses when checking a choice is itself expensive, or when the relationship isn't monotonic so a failed test doesn't tell you which direction to move. Notice that before you start, not two substitutions in.

## Estimation

The no-calculator rule is a promise: the arithmetic in every intended path is tame, so any computation that feels brutal is either the wrong path or an invitation to estimate. Round to friendly numbers and *track the direction of each rounding* — if you rounded $498$ up to $500$ and divided, your estimate is slightly small, and that sign of the error is often exactly what picks between the two nearest choices.

Keep the standing benchmarks loaded: $\tfrac{1}{8} = 12.5\%$, $\tfrac{1}{6} \approx 16.7\%$, $\tfrac{1}{3} \approx 33.3\%$, $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$, $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$, $2^{10} \approx 10^3$. The choice spread sets your precision budget: choices a factor of $2$ apart tolerate rounding everything to one significant figure; choices $4\%$ apart demand you keep a second digit and respect error directions. "Closest to" in the stem is an explicit license to estimate — take it.

## Units digits and magnitude

Two cheap filters catch a remarkable number of wrong choices. The **units digit** of a product or power depends only on the units digits of its inputs, and those cycle with period at most $4$ — so a product ending in $7$ times anything ending in $8$ must end in $6$, and every choice that doesn't is dead without any real multiplication. The **order of magnitude** check is coarser but faster: $6{,}9\text{-something} \times 4{,}1\text{-something}$ is near $28$ million, so choices near $2.8$ million or $280$ million are placeholders for the careless. Run both filters before and after a heavy computation — before, to shrink the field; after, as a two-second audit of your own answer.

## Shape elimination

Some stems determine properties of the answer before they determine the answer: a sum of two odd numbers is even, a quantity defined as "$9$ times an integer" is divisible by $9$, a probability can't exceed $1$, a length can't be negative, a "greater by $40\%$" result must exceed the original. Read the choices through the property and cross out violators — sometimes all four of them. The chapters' trap galleries are full of wrong answers built to fail exactly these checks; the test writers know most students never look.

## The discipline

Choice leverage is a first-class route, not a trick for the unprepared — the exam is explicitly designed so that the strongest scorers mix it freely with algebra. But it obeys the same law as every route: chosen deliberately at $0$:$30$, abandoned decisively at the pace target. Backsolving that needed a third substitution, or estimation against a spread that turned out too tight, is the question raising its price — and the response to a raised price is in *The two-minute decision*, not in your stubbornness.
