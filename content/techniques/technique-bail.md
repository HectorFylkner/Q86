# Strategic Guessing: The Skill That Protects Every Other One

## Why this matters

Twenty-one questions, forty-five minutes. That is about two minutes and eight seconds each, and the exam does not care which questions you spend them on. A section is not lost on the question you cannot do; it is lost on the four you never reached because you refused to abandon it.

This is the one technique with no mathematical content and the largest effect on a score. Every other method here — backsolving, picking numbers, estimating — buys time. This one decides what to do with it. A student who deliberately guesses on two questions and answers the other nineteen carefully will outscore a student who fights all twenty-one and rushes the last six.

The word that matters is *deliberate*. A guess you chose in twenty seconds is a strategy. A guess you were forced into with eleven seconds on the clock is the wreckage of not having one.

## The core ideas

1. **The budget is the section, not the question.** Two minutes and eight seconds is an *average*, not a ration. Some questions deserve fifty seconds, some deserve three minutes, and the only rule that binds is that the total is forty-five.

2. **Checkpoints, not stopwatches.** Watching the clock on every question is a tax on attention. Check it at four points instead, and only compare against a target. On a $21$-question section paced evenly you should have roughly $36$ minutes left entering question $5$, $28$ entering question $9$, $17$ entering question $14$, and $9$ entering question $18$. (Those are $45 - n \times \tfrac{45}{21}$ for $n = 4, 8, 13, 17$, rounded to the nearest minute.)

3. **The ninety-second band.** Within a minute and a half of the target either way, you are on pace and should change nothing. Outside it, act at the *next* question rather than immediately — reacting mid-question is how a recoverable deficit becomes a ruined section.

4. **Recover by guessing forward, never by rushing.** Being three minutes down does not mean doing the next six questions faster. It means picking one hard-looking question and spending twenty seconds on it instead of two minutes. One deliberate guess returns most of a two-minute deficit and costs you a question you were unlikely to get anyway.

5. **The thirty-second read.** Give every question a look before committing: what is it about, does a shortcut exist, do I recognize the shape. If nothing arrives in about thirty seconds, that is information — it is a bail candidate, and the decision is cheapest right now.

6. **The one-minute reassessment.** If you are a minute in with no path to an answer, the expected value of another minute is low and the expected cost is high. Eliminate what you can on properties, take the best remaining choice, and move.

7. **Guess well, not randomly.** A blind guess is one in five. Thirty seconds of elimination on sign, parity, magnitude or units digit routinely gets to one in three or one in two. The guess is not the failure; the *uninformed* guess is.

8. **Two of twenty-one is a normal, healthy number.** Strong scorers commonly abandon a couple of questions per section by choice. Planning for zero abandons is planning to be forced into four.

9. **Sunk cost is the enemy.** Ninety seconds already spent is gone whether you continue or not. The only question is whether the *next* ninety are better spent here or on two questions you can actually finish. The answer is almost always the latter.

10. **Bailing is a trained skill, not a temperament.** It feels like giving up, which is exactly why it has to be rehearsed under a clock until it feels like a move.

## Worked examples

**Example 1**

*You are entering question $9$ of a $21$-question section with $23$ minutes remaining. What should you do?*

*A) Nothing — you are on pace  B) Speed up gradually over the next five questions  C) Take a fast, informed guess on the next question that looks hard  D) Skip ahead to easier questions  E) Guess on the next three questions*

1. Locate the target. Entering question $9$ means eight questions are done, and eight questions of even pacing consume $8 \times \tfrac{45}{21} \approx 17.1$ minutes — so the target is about $27.9$ minutes remaining, call it $27$–$28$.
2. You have $23$, so you are about four to five minutes behind: outside the ninety-second band, and by enough that it will not absorb itself.
3. B is the trap. "Speed up gradually" spreads a five-minute deficit across thirteen questions, which means rushing all thirteen and raising the error rate on questions you would otherwise get right. That converts a timing problem into an accuracy problem.
4. E overcorrects: three guesses buys about five and a half minutes but throws away three questions when you only needed one or two.
5. D is not available — the Focus Quant section has no skip-and-return; you answer in order.
6. C is the move: one deliberate twenty-to-thirty-second guess on a question that already looks expensive returns roughly ninety seconds of buffer at the cost of a question you were unlikely to finish well. Re-check at the next checkpoint and repeat only if still outside the band.

**Answer: C) Take a fast, informed guess on the next question that looks hard**

**Example 2**

*You are ninety seconds into a question. You have set it up correctly but the algebra has turned into a quadratic in two variables and you cannot see the way out. There are $19$ minutes left and $9$ questions remaining. What should you do?*

*A) Finish it — you have already invested the time  B) Start over with a different method  C) Eliminate what you can and guess, then move on  D) Guess immediately without looking at the choices  E) Skip it and come back*

1. First, the pacing facts: $9$ questions in $19$ minutes is about $2$:$07$ each, which is on pace. You are not behind — yet. Spending another ninety seconds here puts you behind by most of a question.
2. A is the sunk-cost error stated plainly. The ninety seconds are gone either way; they buy no discount on the next ninety.
3. B is worse than it sounds. Restarting is only right if a *specific* alternative has occurred to you — a backsolve, a picked number, a bound. "Try again differently" without a named method is just spending the same ninety seconds twice.
4. E is unavailable in this section.
5. Between C and D, the difference is thirty seconds and a doubling of your odds. You have the setup, so you know things about the answer: its sign, roughly its size, perhaps its parity. That is enough to kill two choices.
6. C. Eliminate on the properties you already know, take the best survivor, move on with $19$ minutes and $8$ questions — comfortably ahead of pace again.

**Answer: C) Eliminate what you can and guess, then move on**

**Example 3**

*You reach question $18$ with $11$ minutes remaining. Four questions are left. What is the right plan?*

*A) Two minutes each and accept leaving one blank  B) Answer all four at about $2$:$45$ each  C) Answer all four at about $2$:$00$ each, using the extra minute as buffer  D) Guess the remaining four immediately and finish early  E) Slow down — you are ahead, so use the time*

1. The target entering question $18$ is about $9$ minutes remaining, so with $11$ you are two minutes *ahead* of pace. That is a real asset, not a rounding error.
2. B misreads it. Four questions at $2$:$45$ is $11$ minutes exactly, which spends the entire buffer and leaves nothing for the one question that runs long — and on a $21$-question section, one of the last four usually does.
3. A leaves a question blank for no reason, which is strictly worse than any guess: there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so a blank is a guaranteed zero where a guess is one in five.
4. D throws away four questions you have the time to answer properly. Being ahead is not a reason to stop.
5. E is the subtler trap: "slow down" turns a buffer into a habit of over-checking, and buffers exist to absorb surprises, not to be consumed on purpose.
6. C keeps the pace you have been holding and reserves the extra minute for whichever of the four turns out to be the hard one. If none is, you finish with a minute spare and check your most doubtful answer.

**Answer: C) Answer all four at about $2$:$00$ each, using the extra minute as buffer**

## Trigger cues

- Thirty seconds in with no method in mind → this is a bail candidate; decide now, while the decision is still cheap.
- Ninety seconds in with a correct setup and no route to the answer → eliminate on what the setup already tells you, guess, move.
- A checkpoint shows you more than ninety seconds behind → take one informed guess at the *next* expensive-looking question, then re-check.
- A checkpoint shows you more than ninety seconds ahead → hold the pace and keep the buffer; do not spend it deliberately.
- The question needs arithmetic you know you are slow at (long division, four-digit multiplication) → estimate or bail; slow arithmetic under a clock is where careless errors come from.
- Fewer than thirty seconds left on the section with questions unanswered → answer every remaining one immediately, on any basis; a blank scores zero and a guess does not.
- You have already abandoned two questions and are still behind → the problem is not this section's pacing, it is that the material is above your current level; note it for the post-mortem rather than fighting it now.

## Trap gallery

- **Sunk cost.** "I have already spent two minutes" is an argument for leaving, not for staying. Past time cannot be recovered by spending more.
- **Rushing to recover.** Spreading a deficit across many questions raises the error rate on the ones you would have got. One deliberate guess is cheaper than ten hurried answers.
- **The uninformed guess.** Guessing without eliminating throws away the free thirty seconds that would have doubled your odds.
- **Leaving a blank.** There is no wrong-answer penalty. An unanswered question is strictly worse than a random one.
- **Checking the clock every question.** Attention spent on the timer is attention not spent on the stem. Four checkpoints is enough.
- **Reacting mid-question.** Discovering you are behind and abandoning the question in progress wastes everything already invested in it. Finish or guess this one, then act on the *next*.
- **Treating a buffer as spare time.** Being ahead means you can absorb one hard question later, not that you should slow down now.
- **Planning to abandon nothing.** A plan with no allowance for bailing breaks the first time a question runs long, and then the abandons happen to you instead of being chosen.

## Speed moves

- **Memorize four numbers, not twenty-one.** $36$, $28$, $17$, $9$ — minutes remaining entering questions $5$, $9$, $14$ and $18$. That is the whole pacing system.
- **Decide within thirty seconds whether a question is yours.** The decision is nearly free early and expensive late.
- **Keep a running abandon count.** Two is normal, three is acceptable, four means something else is wrong and the post-mortem needs to know.
- **Eliminate before every guess, without exception.** Sign, parity, magnitude, units digit: one of the four is usually available in fifteen seconds.
- **When you guess, guess and go.** Re-opening an abandoned question later costs the setup time twice.
- **Rehearse bailing in practice.** Under untimed conditions the skill never develops, because nothing forces the choice. Timed sets are the only place it can be trained.

## Before you drill

- I know the four checkpoint numbers ($36$, $28$, $17$, $9$) and the ninety-second band without looking them up.
- I make an explicit solve-or-bail decision within thirty seconds of reading a stem.
- I reassess at one minute and act at ninety seconds when no path has appeared.
- I recover from a deficit by taking one deliberate guess, never by rushing several questions.
- I always eliminate before guessing, and I never leave a question blank.
- I treat two abandons per section as normal rather than as failure.
- I hold my pace when I am ahead instead of spending the buffer.
- I practise abandoning under a clock, because it cannot be learned any other way.
