# The Six Misses: Naming What Went Wrong So You Repair The Right Thing

## Why this matters

Every wrong answer on this platform gets one of six labels, and every one of them sends you somewhere different. A content gap sends you to a chapter's core ideas. A setup error sends you to its trigger cues. A calculation error sends you to its speed moves. Those are three different evenings of work, and picking the wrong one costs you the evening.

That is the whole argument for learning this vocabulary. Not tidiness — routing. A miss you cannot name becomes "I need to do more questions", which is the least specific plan available and the one most likely to reproduce the miss.

There is a second argument, and it is sharper. The six labels are not a taxonomy someone invented for you to memorise. They are the six *repairs*. Ask of any wrong answer: **what would have to change for this to stop happening?** The answer is always one of six things — a rule you did not have, a model you built wrong, arithmetic that slipped, a question you did not read, a clock you lost to, or a method you never committed to. There is no seventh.

The exam does not tell you which one happened. Your own post-mortem is the only place it gets decided, and a label chosen carelessly there is worse than no label, because it sends you confidently to the wrong repair.

## The core ideas

1. **The label is the repair, not the symptom.** Two misses can break the same equation and need opposite fixes. "I got $-14$ instead of $14$" describes the symptom; whether the repair is *learn the sign rule*, *check the setup direction*, or *slow the arithmetic down* is the label. Always name the repair.

2. **Content gap — you did not have the rule.** The method you needed was not available to you, or the version you had was false. "Squares of fractions shrink" is true for $0 < x < 1$ and false at $x = -\tfrac12$, where $x^2 = \tfrac14 > -\tfrac12$. A false general belief is a content gap however confidently it was held. Repair: the chapter's core ideas.

3. **Setup error — you had the rules and arranged them wrongly.** You knew rates add and times do not, and you added the times anyway. You knew percent change is measured against the original, and you divided by the new price. Nothing was missing; the model was wrong. Repair: the chapter's trigger cues, which are the "when you see this, reach for that" table.

4. **Calculation error — the model was right and the execution slipped.** A dropped sign, a misplaced decimal, an off-by-one inside a division. The distinguishing test is that if you redid the same steps carefully you would get the right answer. Repair: the chapter's speed moves, which exist to remove the steps where slips happen.

5. **Misread — you answered a different question.** Your work was correct *for some other well-posed question*. You solved for $60 - x$ when $x$ was asked. You reported the sum when the seventh term was asked. You used the ratio's larger part when the smaller was wanted. This is the miss that feels most unfair and is the most preventable. Repair: the trap gallery, because misread-shaped distractors are exactly what the traps are built from.

6. **Time pressure — the method was abandoned to the clock.** Not "I was slow", which is usually a content gap wearing a costume. Time pressure means you knew what to do, started doing it, and stopped because the clock made you. Repair: pacing and the abandon rules, not more content.

7. **Guess — no method was committed to at all.** You picked something. This is a legitimate and sometimes correct decision, and it is only a defect if it was forced rather than chosen. Repair: the chapter, if the material was reachable; the bail rules, if it was not.

8. **A long wrong answer is almost never time pressure.** Spending three minutes and getting it wrong means the time went somewhere that did not work — that is a content gap or a setup error. Time pressure produces *fast* wrong answers. The two repairs are opposites, and confusing them is the single most expensive mislabel available: it tells you to speed up when what you needed was to learn the method.

9. **A confident miss is a belief, not a slip.** If you locked the answer in, attention did not wander; the method itself was wrong. That points at content or setup and away from calculation. Conversely, a miss on a subtopic you score $85\%$ on is far likelier to be execution than knowledge.

10. **The distractor you chose is evidence, not decoration.** Every wrong choice in this bank is *proved* to be the number a specific named mistake produces. Which wrong answer you picked is therefore a direct observation about which mistake you made — usually a better one than your memory of what you were thinking.

11. **When two labels both fit, the sentence's main verb decides.** "Assumes the median cannot be found without knowing the set's size" is a belief about a rule, so content gap. "Reverses the weights, putting weight $18$ on the average $8$" is an arrangement, so setup. Do not average the two; pick the one whose repair you would actually do tomorrow.

## Worked examples

**Example 1**

*A question asks for the speed of a boat in still water. You work out the downstream and upstream speeds correctly as $15$ and $9$ km/h, then answer $15$. Which label?*

*A) Content gap  B) Setup error  C) Calculation error  D) Misread  E) Time pressure*

1. Check the arithmetic first, because if it is wrong the label may be C. Downstream $= b + c$, upstream $= b - c$. With $b + c = 15$ and $b - c = 9$: adding gives $2b = 24$, so $b = 12$; subtracting gives $2c = 6$, so $c = 3$. Check: $12 + 3 = 15$ and $12 - 3 = 9$. The two extracted speeds are correct.
2. So the work was right up to the last step. That rules out C, and it rules out A — the method for combining a current with a still-water speed was clearly available.
3. Is it B? A setup error would mean the *model* was wrong — adding times instead of rates, say. Here the model produced $15$ and $9$ correctly.
4. What went wrong is that $15$ is the answer to "what is the downstream speed", not "what is the speed in still water". The work is correct for a different, perfectly well-posed question. That is the definition of D.
5. And note the repair this routes you to: not more rate problems, but the habit of writing down what is asked before solving. The trap gallery is full of exactly this shape, which is why misread routes there.

**Answer: D) Misread**

**Example 2**

*A question gives a price that rises $25\%$ and then falls $20\%$. You compute $25 - 20 = 5$ and answer "a $5\%$ net rise". Which label?*

*A) Content gap  B) Setup error  C) Calculation error  D) Misread  E) Guess*

1. First get the truth. Successive changes multiply: $1.25 \times 0.80$. Compute it as a fraction to avoid a decimal slip — $\tfrac54 \times \tfrac45 = \tfrac{20}{20} = 1$. The net change is exactly $0\%$, not $5\%$.
2. Is it C? A calculation error would mean the method was right and a number slipped. But $25 - 20 = 5$ is arithmetically correct — the subtraction is fine. What is wrong is that subtraction was the operation at all.
3. Is it D? A misread would mean you answered a different well-posed question. "What is $25 - 20$" is not the question anyone asked, and $5\%$ is not the correct answer to any reading of this stem.
4. Between A and B: is this a missing rule, or a rule you had and mis-arranged? The belief being acted on is *percent changes add* — a general claim, applied here and everywhere. It is not a mis-arrangement of a rule you hold; it is the wrong rule.
5. So A. The repair is the core ideas of percent change chains, where the multiplier form is established, and not "be more careful", which would have changed nothing.

**Answer: A) Content gap**

**Example 3**

*On a subtopic where your last twenty attempts are $17$ correct, you spend $40$ seconds on a $2$-minute question, mark it "lean", and get it wrong by dropping a minus sign in the third line. Which label?*

*A) Content gap  B) Setup error  C) Calculation error  D) Misread  E) Time pressure*

1. Take the evidence in order. Recent record on this subtopic: $17/20 = 85\%$. That is strong, so the method is very unlikely to be missing — A and B both become improbable.
2. Time: $40$ seconds against a $120$-second benchmark is a ratio of $\tfrac{40}{120} = \tfrac13$, under half. That is fast enough that E is on the table.
3. But E has a specific meaning: the method was abandoned *because of the clock*. Nothing here says the clock was involved — this was one question, answered quickly because it felt familiar, not a section running out.
4. And the miss is named precisely: a dropped minus sign in the third line. The model was right, the steps were right, one sign went missing. That is C by definition — redo the same steps carefully and the answer comes out right.
5. The confidence tag supports it. "Lean" rather than "lock" is consistent with a slip rather than a wrong belief; a locked-in miss on an $85\%$ subtopic would have been more surprising and would have deserved a second look at the setup.
6. Repair: the chapter's speed moves — the point of which is to eliminate the hand-written steps where signs get lost.

**Answer: C) Calculation error**

## Trigger cues

- Your work is correct for a *different* well-posed question → misread, every time; do not soften it to "careless".
- You can state the general rule you were acting on, and the rule is false → content gap, regardless of how confident you were.
- You held the right rules and combined them the wrong way round → setup error.
- Redoing the identical steps carefully would fix it → calculation error.
- Long *and* wrong → content gap or setup error; time pressure produces fast wrong answers, not slow ones.
- Fast *and* wrong, with a condition from the stem unused → misread before time pressure; check what you skipped before blaming the clock.
- You marked it "lock" and missed it → look at content and setup first; a confident miss is a wrong belief, not a lapse of attention.
- You marked it "guess" before submitting → guess, and then the real question is *why* no method arrived — that is the thing to note.
- The subtopic sits above $80\%$ in your recent record → weight execution over knowledge.
- The subtopic sits below $50\%$ → weight knowledge over execution; this is a pattern, not an accident.

## Trap gallery

- **"Careless" as a category.** It is not one of the six because it is not a repair. Almost every miss filed as careless is a calculation error or a misread, and those two have different fixes.
- **Calling a slow miss time pressure.** The clock did not cause it; not having a method did. Filing it as time pressure prescribes speed, which makes the next attempt worse.
- **Calling a misread a content gap.** The most common over-diagnosis. It sends you to re-read a chapter you already know and leaves the actual habit — naming the quantity asked for — untouched.
- **Calling a content gap a calculation error.** The most comfortable mislabel, because it implies you nearly had it. It also guarantees the miss repeats.
- **Labelling from memory rather than from the work.** What you remember thinking is reconstructed after the fact. The distractor you chose and the time you took are contemporaneous.
- **Accepting the platform's suggestion without reading it.** The suggestion is inferred from timing, confidence, the subtopic record and the distractor. It is often right and it is not authoritative — and when you overrule it, that disagreement is the only data that can ever improve it.
- **Using "guess" to mean "not sure".** Guess means no method was committed to. A method you were unsure of is still a method, and its failure has one of the other five names.
- **One label for a session.** Six misses in a set can have six different causes. A single verdict for the whole run is a mood, not a diagnosis.

## Speed moves

- **Ask the repair question first.** "What would have to change for this to stop happening?" gets to the label faster than trying to remember the definitions.
- **Read your own distractor.** Every wrong choice here is built from a named mistake. Open the solution and see which one you walked into; it is usually the answer.
- **Use the time ratio as a filter, not a verdict.** Under half the benchmark points at misread or time pressure; over double points at content or setup. Then decide on the work.
- **Let the confidence tag break ties.** Lock pushes toward content and setup; guess pushes toward guess; lean leaves it open.
- **Check the subtopic's recent record before choosing between knowledge and execution.** It is one number and it settles most of these.
- **Tag it inside the same minute.** The evidence you need is on the screen now and reconstructed later.

## Before you drill

- I can name all six labels and the repair each one prescribes, without looking them up.
- I ask "what would have to change" rather than "what feels right" when labelling.
- I never file a slow wrong answer as time pressure.
- I check whether my work was correct for a different question before reaching for any other label.
- I read the distractor I chose and what it was built from.
- I let a locked-in miss push me toward content and setup rather than carelessness.
- I overrule the suggestion when I disagree with it, knowing the disagreement is what improves it.
