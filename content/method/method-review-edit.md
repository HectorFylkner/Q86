# Review & Edit: Three Changes, And How To Spend Them

## Why this matters

The Focus Edition gives you something the old exam did not: at the end of a section you may go back, and you may change up to **three** answers, using whatever time is left on that section's clock. Bookmarking is unlimited and free; the edits are not.

Three is a small number and it is the whole design. It is not "go back and check your work" — there is not enough time for that, and there is not enough edit budget for it either. It is a scalpel for a specific situation: you finished a question knowing something was off, and you want one more look.

The failure mode is not using the feature. It is using it as reassurance. An answer you arrived at by a method, and then changed because it "feels wrong" on a second glance with ninety seconds left, is an answer you are changing on strictly *less* information than you had the first time — you had the working then, and you have a feeling now. That is how a scoring feature turns into a scoring leak.

This chapter is about spending three edits well. The platform's timed sections implement the mechanic exactly — three changes, a reason, and a written justification — so it can be rehearsed rather than met for the first time on exam day.

## The core ideas

1. **Bookmarks are free; edits are scarce.** Bookmark generously *during* the section — it costs nothing and takes a second. Decide what to edit only at the end, when you know how much time and how many candidates you actually have.

2. **Bookmark with a reason, not a feeling.** "Unsure" is not a reason, because at the end of a section a third of the questions will feel unsure. Bookmark when something specific happened: you guessed between two survivors, you noticed the units late, you did not finish the check you meant to do.

3. **The budget is three, and the expected number worth using is often fewer.** An edit only pays when the probability that the new answer is right exceeds the probability that the current one is. Changing to break a tie you have already broken once, with less time and no new working, does not clear that bar.

4. **A specific error is the entry ticket.** The test is whether you can complete the sentence *"I answered $X$ because I did $Y$, and $Y$ is wrong because…"*. If the sentence does not finish, close the question. This is why the platform makes you write a justification before it will commit a change: the requirement is not bureaucracy, it is the test itself, and typing it is how you find out you cannot pass it.

5. **The three legitimate reasons.** A calculation error you can point to. A misread you can quote from the stem. A new solution path that produces a *different* answer and that you can execute in the time you have. Anything else — symmetry, a hunch, the answer looking too round — is not a reason.

6. **A new path is only a reason if you finish it.** "I now think I should have used the complement" is a thought, not a result. If you cannot carry it to a number before the clock runs out, you are choosing between an answer you derived and an answer you imagined.

7. **Re-derive, do not re-read.** Reading your own working again mostly reproduces the original error, because the error is in how you read it. If you are going to spend an edit, redo the step by a different route — the fraction instead of the decimal, the complement instead of the direct count.

8. **Spend the budget on your own error profile.** If your misses are mostly misreads, the highest-yield review is re-reading the *question sentence* of your bookmarked items and nothing else. If they are mostly calculation errors, redo one step. The six-label vocabulary is what tells you which of those you are.

9. **Time is a hard constraint on which edits are even possible.** Two minutes left and three bookmarks is not three edits; it is one careful one. Rank the bookmarks by how cheaply the doubt can be resolved, not by how much they bother you.

10. **An unanswered question is never a legitimate edit target — it is an emergency.** Fill every blank before reviewing anything, because a blank scores zero and a guess does not, and the fix costs five seconds.

11. **Your own ledger is the arbiter.** Every change you make in a timed section here is recorded with what it did: rescued a wrong answer, destroyed a right one, or moved nothing. After a handful of sections that record tells you whether your edits pay, and which of your three stated reasons pays best. General advice about editing is worth less than your own dozen rows.

## Worked examples

**Example 1**

*A $21$-question section is over and you have $3$ minutes $30$ seconds left with four bookmarks. Bookmark A: you guessed between two choices. Bookmark B: you are fairly sure you used the wrong base for a percent change. Bookmark C: the answer came out negative and you expected positive. Bookmark D: it felt hard. Where do the edits go?*

*A) A, B, C — use all three  B) B and C  C) B only  D) C and D  E) None; the section is done*

1. Budget the time before choosing. Four bookmarks in $210$ seconds is just over $52$ seconds each if you open all four, which is not enough to redo any of them. Opening fewer and finishing is worth more than opening all and finishing none.
2. D is out immediately: "it felt hard" completes no sentence of the form "I did $Y$ and $Y$ is wrong". It is a feeling about difficulty, not a located error.
3. A is out for the same reason at one remove. You guessed between two survivors *with the working in front of you*. Guessing again now, from memory, is a coin flip with worse information — it cannot raise the probability of being right.
4. B is a textbook edit. "Wrong base for a percent change" names the error, names the fix, and is fast: recompute the one division against the original rather than the new value. Perhaps $40$ seconds.
5. C is also legitimate but is a different kind. A sign that contradicts an expectation is evidence something specific went wrong, and re-deriving the sign is cheap — check the direction of one subtraction. Call it $60$ seconds, and if it does not resolve, leave the answer as it was rather than changing it on the strength of the surprise alone.
6. B and C together take about $100$ of the $210$ seconds, leaving margin. Three edits were available and two were earned; the third is not a prize for spending it.

**Answer: B) B and C**

**Example 2**

*You have made two edits and $50$ seconds remain. A third bookmarked question is one where you now believe a faster method — counting the complement instead of counting directly — would apply. You have not carried it out. Do you edit?*

*A) Yes — the complement is the right method  B) Yes, if the direct count looked long  C) No, unless you can carry the complement to a number in the time left  D) No — never change on a new method  E) Yes, and change to whichever choice is nearest your original*

1. The claim on the table is that a different method applies. That is not yet a claim that your answer is wrong: two methods that both work agree.
2. So the only thing that justifies the edit is a *different number* at the end of the new method. Until it produces one, there is nothing to change to. E is nonsense for the same reason — proximity to a wrong answer is not evidence.
3. Can it be carried out? Fifty seconds is enough for a complement count on a small set: total minus the excluded cases, one subtraction. It is not enough for a complement over a case split with three branches.
4. So the answer is conditional, which is exactly what C says. If the complement lands on a number inside the time, you have a real disagreement between two derivations and the newer, cleaner one is the better bet. If it does not, you are choosing between a derived answer and an unfinished intention.
5. D is too strong. "New solution path" is one of the three legitimate reasons — but it is legitimate as a *completed* path, not as a realisation.

**Answer: C) No, unless you can carry the complement to a number in the time left**

**Example 3**

*Across your recorded timed sections you have made $16$ edits: $11$ turned a wrong answer right, $0$ turned a right answer wrong, and $5$ changed one wrong answer for another. What does this tell you, and what should change?*

*A) Editing is costing you points; edit less  B) Editing is paying; the discipline is working, and the $5$ are the thing to look at  C) The sample is too small to say anything  D) You should raise your edit count to three every section  E) It says nothing because the $5$ were neutral*

1. Compute the net first. Rescued minus destroyed is $11 - 0 = +11$ questions. That is unambiguous in direction, so A is simply false for this reader whatever is true in general.
2. Is it too small to read? Sixteen is small for a percentage but the *zero* is informative on its own: across sixteen chances to damage a right answer, it happened no times. That is a discipline working, not a coin landing.
3. E misreads the neutrals. Wrong-to-wrong is not "no information" — it is $5$ occasions where a real error was correctly identified and the replacement was still wrong. That points at the second half of the edit, the re-derivation, not at the decision to open the question.
4. D is the trap, and it is the one this chapter exists to prevent. A good record does not mean the budget should be filled. The $11$ came from edits that had a located error; manufacturing a third edit in a section where only one error was located is how the $0$ becomes a $1$.
5. So B. The behaviour to keep is the entry test; the thing to work on is the $5$ — most likely by re-deriving rather than re-reading, so the second attempt does not repeat the first one's mistake.

**Answer: B) Editing is paying; the discipline is working, and the $5$ are the thing to look at**

## Trigger cues

- You finished a question and something specific bothered you → bookmark it now, with the reason, and move on.
- You finished a question feeling generally unsure → do not bookmark; that describes a third of the section and will drown the real candidates.
- The section ends and a question is unanswered → fill it before opening any bookmark; a blank is a guaranteed zero.
- You can complete "I did $Y$ and $Y$ is wrong because…" → this is an edit candidate.
- You cannot complete that sentence → close the question, whatever the feeling.
- A sign, a unit, or an order of magnitude contradicts what the stem implies → strong candidate; these are cheap to re-derive and are where real errors hide.
- You want to change a guess to the other survivor → do not; you had more information the first time.
- Fewer than $60$ seconds remain → at most one edit, and only if the fix is a single step.
- You have already committed three changes → the section is closed; use the remaining time to check the blanks, not the answers.

## Trap gallery

- **Editing for reassurance.** Opening a question to feel better about it, then changing something to justify having opened it. The budget gets spent on the questions that made you anxious rather than the ones that were wrong.
- **The second guess.** Changing a coin-flip to the other side. It was $50/50$ with the working in front of you; it is $50/50$ now with less.
- **Re-reading your own work.** The error lives in how you read the stem or executed the step, and reading it again reproduces it. Re-derive by a different route or leave it.
- **Trading down on a hunch.** Abandoning a derived answer for one that "looks more like an answer" — rounder, smaller, more central among the choices. The choices were built to make that feel true.
- **Filling the budget.** Treating three as a target. The number of edits worth making is set by how many located errors you have, which is often one or zero.
- **Spending the clock on the first bookmark.** Opening them in the order you bookmarked them rather than in order of how cheaply the doubt resolves.
- **Editing an unanswered question instead of answering it.** The blank is a certain zero and takes five seconds to fix; the edit is a maybe.
- **Ignoring your own ledger.** The platform records what every one of your edits did. A reader who has never looked is taking general advice over their own evidence.

## Speed moves

- **Bookmark in one second, with a word.** "Base?", "sign", "units". Enough to reconstruct the doubt at the end without re-reading the whole question.
- **Fill blanks first, always.** It is the only move on this screen with a guaranteed positive expectation.
- **Sort bookmarks by cost, not by anxiety.** A one-division fix beats a three-branch case split when the clock is the binding constraint.
- **Redo the step in a different form.** Fractions instead of decimals, complement instead of direct, a number picked instead of the algebra. A second pass by the same route mostly re-runs the first.
- **Write the justification before you change the answer.** If it will not write, it is not an edit. This is why the platform demands one before it commits.
- **Stop when the located errors run out.** The unused edits are not wasted; they are the ones that did not damage anything.
- **Read your ledger after every few sections.** Rescued, destroyed, and which reason produced which. It is the only edit advice that is actually about you.

## Before you drill

- I bookmark with a specific reason and never with "unsure".
- I fill every blank before I open a single bookmark.
- I can state the located error in a full sentence before I change anything.
- I re-derive by a different route rather than re-reading my own working.
- I never change a considered guess to the other survivor.
- I treat three as a ceiling, not a target, and I stop when the located errors run out.
- I rank bookmarks by how cheaply the doubt resolves, not by how much it nags.
- I read my own edit ledger — rescued, destroyed, and by reason — instead of relying on general advice.
